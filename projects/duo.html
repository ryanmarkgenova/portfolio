Tap to Approve

Do students actually understand Duo and MFA?

Every UW student taps "Approve" in Duo dozens of times a quarter. We wanted to know if they understand what it protects them from, and whether convenience and trust decide if they'd use MFA on their own. We surveyed students, cleaned and analyzed the data, interviewed people on campus and a UW IT support manager, and turned it into a research paper and a short video to teach our peers.

01 · The problem

Tapping approve isn't knowing

Universities are big targets for phishing and stolen passwords, and MFA is one of the best defenses. But it only works if people actually use it, and students often see Duo as one more annoying step.

Our two questions: How well do students understand the risks MFA protects against? And how do convenience, trust and ease of use shape whether they'd adopt it?

02 · Method

Survey, clean, code

  1. Survey. A 10-question Google Form: five knowledge checks, three attitude questions and two open-ended ones, sent through class group chats. 20 valid responses across majors and class years.
  2. Clean. Removed duplicate, incomplete and joke answers, and standardized messy text like "first year," "freshman" and "1st yr" into one class standing.
  3. Code. Turned open-ended answers into numbers: 0/1 for yes/no items, and a 1 to 5 scale for confidence, cross-checked between teammates.
  4. Analyze. PivotTables comparing subgroups, like class standing vs MFA awareness, and understanding vs trust.
  5. Interview. On-camera interviews with students around campus, two faculty members and a UW IT support manager, to explain the numbers.

Why 20 people? This was an exploratory, pattern-finding study, not a statistical survey. Usability research norms (Nielsen Norman Group) show core patterns show up with small groups, so we scoped it to find misconceptions worth a bigger study.

03 · Findings

Trust without understanding

Most students trust MFA, but not because they know how it works. Convenience, not knowledge, decides whether they use it.

What people told us

  • Convenience wins. 12 of 20 would rather use a different method like a text code. Students who liked Duo liked it because "it takes like two seconds… I use my fingerprint."
  • Skipping on personal accounts. Many admitted skipping MFA outside school out of laziness or "forgetting," even when they knew it helped.
  • Exposure changes minds. Students who had seen a phishing attempt or a suspicious login were much more supportive of mandatory MFA.
  • Faculty saw it differently. Two lecturers worried less about the tap and more about privacy and data sharing, calling MFA "an arms race."

"The misconception might be that it's more pain than the… pain-benefit ratio. I'm not sure they see the benefit in it."

UW IT support manager

04 · The video

Teaching it on camera

A paper only reaches the people who grade it, so I made a short hook video for students. I went around campus asking people what Duo actually does, then cut their answers against an interview with a UW IT support manager, who explained MFA as "something you know and something you have," so a stolen password isn't enough on its own.

05 · What I learned

Security is a people problem

The tech works. What breaks it is friction and misunderstanding. If MFA feels like a chore, people skip it, so security teams have to design for convenience and explain the why, not just require it.

Coding open-ended answers taught me how much judgment hides in "clean" data. We cross-checked each other's codes to keep that subjectivity in check, and we were upfront about the limits: 20 people, self-reported, one point in time.
